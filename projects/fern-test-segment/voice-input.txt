A cockroach is walking through dry leaves — not where it wants to go, but where it is led, by one of its own antennae, cut short. Holding what is left of it is a wasp, twenty-two millimetres, metallic green, walking her catch home to a burrow, to be eaten alive, slowly, over about eight days, by a larva that has not hatched yet. The cockroach is about twice her length.

First came a fight: the cockroach reared, kicked, bit — and cockroaches sometimes win. This one lost. The first sting went into its chest, and for two to three minutes its front legs failed. The second sting took longer: through the neck, into the head — not a jab but a search, her stinger tip feeling for one knot of nerve until she found it.

What it did next is strange. For about thirty minutes it cleaned itself — antennae, legs, every surface — while the wasp was elsewhere. Then she came back, cut the antennae to stumps, and drank from them. She took one stump, pulled, and the cockroach walked: on its own legs, in her direction. That is the walk we came in on.

Nothing in it is broken. It can right itself, swim, and — thrown into the air — fly. The machinery is whole — and it walks behind the animal that will seal it in a burrow alive. Something was taken out of it, inside its head, and everything you have seen is that, from outside. Let's go back, to the two stings — and to the one thing the second one took.
